Bridge painting is one of the most demanding specialties in the industrial coatings industry. The work itself is physically challenging — swing stages, elevated scaffolding, work over traffic and water. The regulatory environment is complex — AMPP/SSPC standards, lead paint protocols, environmental containment requirements. And the insurance requirements are more demanding than virtually any other coating specialty.

This guide covers what bridge painting contractors need to know about insurance — from DOT additional insured requirements to professional liability for long service life specifications.

## Why Bridge Painting Insurance Is Different

Several factors make bridge painting contractor insurance more complex than general coating contractor coverage:

**Lead paint.** Most bridges built before 1980 have lead-containing paint. Abrasive blasting operations disturb that paint, creating lead exposure risk for workers and lead contamination risk for the environment. Workers compensation, contractors pollution liability, and EPA/OSHA compliance all come into play.

**Height and fall exposure.** Bridge painting crews work at height — on swing stages over traffic, on scaffolding over waterways, on elevated structures accessible only by crane or lift. Fall injuries are a significant workers compensation exposure.

**Environmental sensitivity.** Many bridges span navigable waterways, wetlands, or areas with environmental sensitivity. Containment failures create significant pollution liability exposure. CPL is essential for bridge painting contractors.

**Long service life specifications.** A DOT bridge painting contract may specify a 20 or 25-year anti-corrosion service life. If the coating system fails to meet that specification, the re-coating cost for a major span can be millions of dollars. Professional liability coverage for that exposure is critical.

**DOT insurance requirements.** State transportation departments impose specific insurance requirements in bridge painting contracts — minimum limits, specific AI endorsement forms, primary/non-contributory language, and umbrella requirements that exceed what most coatings contractors carry for facility work.

## General Liability for Bridge Painting

Your GL policy covers the core third-party liability exposure from bridge painting work:

- Third-party bodily injury at the project site
- Third-party property damage from your operations
- Completed operations — claims arising after the project is finished
- Defense costs for covered claims

For bridge painting contractors, two GL issues require specific attention:

**Completed operations coverage.** Bridge painting contracts specify service life — often 15 to 25 years. Completed operations coverage in your GL needs to extend for a comparable period. If your GL completed operations coverage is limited to two or three years, you have a gap in your program relative to your contract obligations.

**Additional insured endorsements.** State DOTs typically require specific AI endorsement forms. The most common requirement is ISO CG 2010 (for ongoing operations) and CG 2037 (for completed operations), plus primary and non-contributory language. Some DOTs have their own required AI language that differs from standard ISO forms. We verify your policy meets the specific DOT requirements before you bid.

## Contractors Pollution Liability for Bridge Painters

CPL is mandatory for bridge painting contractors. Here is why:

Your GL pollution exclusion will deny coverage for:
- Lead paint contamination from abrasive blasting operations
- Blast media, paint chip, and rust scale releases from containment failures
- Chemical coating material releases (solvent vapors, coating spills)
- Wash water contamination from surface cleaning operations

CPL explicitly covers these events:
- Third-party bodily injury from lead paint exposure to workers from other trades and bystanders
- Property damage from blast media or paint chip contamination of adjacent property or waterways
- Cleanup costs for containment failures
- Defense costs for pollution-related claims

For bridge painting contractors, CPL limits should be calibrated to the scale of your projects and the environmental sensitivity of your work areas. Operations over navigable waterways, near drinking water intakes, or in ecologically sensitive areas warrant higher CPL limits.

**Lead paint CPL.** Some carriers specifically exclude or limit coverage for lead paint disturbance. We verify that your CPL policy covers lead paint disturbance operations before binding — this is a non-negotiable element of a bridge painting contractor program.

## Professional Liability for Service Life Specifications

Bridge painting professional liability is a specialized product that addresses the unique risk of long-duration performance commitments.

When a DOT specifies that a bridge coating system must achieve a 20-year service life to first maintenance, they are creating a long-duration professional liability exposure for the contractor who applies the coating. If the coating fails at year 7, the contractor faces a claim that may include:

- Re-blasting and re-coating of the bridge span (potentially $1M to $10M or more depending on span length and coating system)
- Traffic disruption costs during re-work
- Inspection and testing costs
- Expert witness and legal fees

Professional liability covers the defense costs and damages from these claims. For bridge painting contractors, professional liability is as important as GL — the potential claim size on a service life specification failure can be existential for a mid-size contractor.

**Claims-made coverage and tail.** Bridge painting E&O is claims-made. You need active coverage throughout the service life warranty period. When you eventually stop operating, you need a tail policy that extends coverage for the remaining warranty period. Plan for this in your insurance budget.

**Documentation as defense.** The best professional liability defense is thorough documentation: daily inspection records, DFT measurements, surface preparation grade documentation, ambient condition logs, and product application records. We encourage bridge painting contractors to implement formal documentation programs — both to reduce claim risk and to improve their position with professional liability underwriters.

## Workers Compensation for Bridge Painting Crews

Bridge painting workers compensation covers several specific injury patterns:

**Lead exposure.** OSHA's Lead Standard for Construction (29 CFR 1926.62) imposes blood lead level monitoring, respiratory protection, hygiene controls, and medical surveillance requirements for workers exposed to lead above the action level. Workers comp covers workers who develop lead toxicity despite these controls.

**Falls from elevation.** Swing stage, scaffold, and lift work creates significant fall risk. Fatal and serious fall injuries are the most consequential workers comp claims in bridge painting. Fall protection, OSHA compliance, and rigorous equipment inspection programs reduce this risk — but workers comp is there when falls occur.

**Musculoskeletal injuries.** Blast gun operation, spray application, and hand-tool work at elevation create back, shoulder, and arm injuries.

**Hearing loss.** Abrasive blasting equipment and compressors create sustained noise exposure. Hearing protection compliance is essential; occupational hearing loss claims are still common in the industry.

Workers comp class codes for bridge painting may differ by state — some states have specific codes for structural steel painting or bridge painting that carry rates different from general painting codes. Correct classification is important for premium accuracy.

## Umbrella Limits for DOT Projects

Bridge painting DOT contracts typically require higher total liability capacity than most other coatings work. Common umbrella requirements:

- State DOT highway bridge projects: $5M to $10M total
- Major span projects over navigable waterways: $10M to $20M
- Federal infrastructure projects: varies by contract

Umbrella coverage provides these limits above your primary GL and auto at a fraction of the cost of equivalent primary limits. For a bridge painting contractor, umbrella is a bid requirement, not optional coverage.
