For most contractors, professional liability (errors and omissions) insurance is primarily associated with design professionals — architects, engineers, consultants who provide professional services. But for industrial coatings contractors working on major infrastructure, professional liability is equally relevant — and often equally necessary.

This guide explains when and why industrial coatings contractors need professional liability, what it covers, and how to structure coverage for infrastructure and facility work.

## When Professional Liability Applies to Coatings Work

Professional liability applies to any claim that alleges your professional judgment, recommendations, or methodology caused a financial loss. For industrial coatings contractors, these claims arise most often from:

**Coating system selection.** When you recommend a coating system — or when the coating system you apply is specified to achieve a particular performance level — and that system fails, the project owner may claim your professional judgment was the cause. This is especially common when the contractor provided coating system input, selected products within a specification range, or warranted performance.

**Surface preparation methodology.** AMPP/SSPC surface preparation standards are specified in coating contracts. If your surface preparation doesn't achieve the specified grade, and the coating subsequently fails, a professional liability claim will focus on your preparation methodology.

**Application condition compliance.** Temperature, humidity, dew point, and surface temperature requirements are professional obligations in a coating specification. Documented violations of application conditions become the basis for professional liability claims when coating systems fail.

**Specification interpretation.** Complex coating specifications sometimes have ambiguities. How you interpret and apply those specifications — the judgment calls you make in the field — can become professional liability claims when your interpretation is disputed.

**Product recommendations.** When you recommend a specific product within a specification, or when you advise a client on product selection, that recommendation becomes a professional service. If the product fails to perform, you may face a claim based on your recommendation.

## The Service Life Dimension of Infrastructure Coatings

For industrial coatings contractors on infrastructure projects, professional liability exposure has a distinctive time dimension: long service life specifications.

A standard bridge coating specification might require:
- Surface preparation to SSPC SP-10 (Near-White Metal Blast)
- Zinc-rich primer to specified DFT
- High-build epoxy intermediate coat
- Polyurethane topcoat
- Specified service life of 20 years to first maintenance

If the coating system fails at year 6, the project owner faces re-blasting and re-coating costs for a major bridge span. Those costs may be $3M, $10M, or more. The professional liability claim will allege that the contractor's preparation, application, or methodology was deficient — that if the work had been done correctly to specification, the system would have achieved its specified service life.

This creates a professional liability exposure that extends 20 years from project completion. The claim can come years after you finished the work — which is why:
1. Claims-made coverage requires continuous active coverage or a tail
2. Documentation of what you did, how you did it, and the conditions under which you worked becomes a multi-decade obligation

## What Professional Liability Covers for Coatings Contractors

Professional liability insurance covers:

**Defense costs.** Coating system failure claims on infrastructure projects are technically complex and contested. Expert witnesses in coating science, surface preparation engineering, and coating product performance are standard in these cases. Defense costs of $100,000 to $500,000 or more before settlement are not uncommon. Professional liability covers these costs.

**Damages.** When a professional liability claim results in a settlement or judgment, your E&O policy covers the damages up to your policy limit. For infrastructure coating failures, damages typically include re-coating costs, traffic disruption, inspection, and testing costs.

**Pre-claim assistance.** Many professional liability carriers provide pre-claim assistance when a situation is developing but before a formal claim is filed. This can include attorney consultation and early investigation — often the most cost-effective point to address a developing coating failure dispute.

## What Professional Liability Does Not Cover

**Your own re-work obligations.** If you have a contract warranty to re-coat defective work at your own cost, that re-work obligation is not covered by professional liability insurance. E&O covers third-party claims against you, not your own contract performance obligations.

**Bodily injury and property damage.** A coating failure that results in a structural failure causing injury is a GL claim, not an E&O claim. Professional liability focuses on pure financial loss from professional errors, not physical harm.

**Intentional misconduct.** Deliberate falsification of inspection records, intentional misrepresentation of surface preparation quality, or knowing violations of specifications are not covered.

## Claims-Made Coverage: The Critical Mechanic

Professional liability insurance is written on a claims-made basis — unlike GL, which is occurrence-based.

**Occurrence-based (like GL):** The policy in effect when the incident happened responds — even if the claim is filed years later.

**Claims-made (like E&O):** The policy in effect when the claim is filed responds — regardless of when the work was done.

For infrastructure coatings contractors with 20-year service life specifications, this creates a practical challenge: you need professional liability coverage throughout the warranty period, or you need a tail policy that extends coverage beyond policy cancellation.

When an industrial coatings contractor retires, sells, or closes operations, their professional liability exposure doesn't end — it runs until the warranty period on their last major project expires. Planning for this requires either:
1. Purchasing tail coverage (extended reporting period) at the time of cancellation, or
2. Continuing E&O in force throughout the warranty period

## Documentation: Your Most Important Professional Liability Defense

The best defense against a professional liability claim is documentation that proves you performed the work correctly.

For infrastructure coatings contractors, essential documentation includes:

**Daily inspection records.** Daily logs recording: surface cleanliness before coating, surface profile measurements (anchor profile), surface temperature, ambient temperature, relative humidity, dew point, and the margin between surface temperature and dew point. These records prove you applied the coating at proper conditions.

**Dry film thickness records.** DFT measurements for each coat, in sufficient quantity to represent the coated area, recorded with location and date. These prove the coating was applied at the specified DFT.

**Product application records.** Batch numbers, mixing ratios, pot life, and application date/time for each application. These prove you used the specified products, mixed them correctly, and applied them within pot life.

**Pre-application photographs.** Document surface condition before coating — cleanliness level, visible surface defects, any deviations from specification noted and reported.

**Third-party inspection records.** For major infrastructure projects, third-party inspection by an AMPP/SSPC-certified coating inspector creates an independent record of your work. This is among the strongest professional liability defenses available.

**Client communication records.** Emails, RFIs, and formal communications documenting coating specification questions, client approvals, and any deviations from specification approved by the project owner.

These records may need to be produced and used in litigation 10 or 15 years after project completion. Maintain them accordingly — in formats that will be accessible over the long term.
